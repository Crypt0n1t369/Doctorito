# Atbalsts: radio capacity and private message channels

13 September 2026 · Engineering estimate for the [two-station Latvian reference pilot](atbalsts-radio-reference-build-2026-09-13.md). **No off-air FM, LoRaWAN, licensed-radio or cryptographic interoperability field test is claimed.** Recalculate every table with [the executable model](atbalsts-private-radio-capacity-2026-09-13.py). The numeric results describe an allocated audio slot and surveyed data rates, not blanket capacity for a municipality.

## Decision

**Private content over publicly receivable radio is technically realistic.** Encrypt the application object on the sending phone or at the centre for a recipient's *pre-registered public key*; let FM, the community station and LoRaWAN carry only opaque bytes; decrypt in the recipient's app with that recipient's private key. Sign centre-originated ciphertext so the app can distinguish it from a forged message. A frequency or LoRaWAN channel is not a privacy boundary, and creating more logical channels does not add radio airtime. [HPKE RFC 9180](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9180.html), [COSE RFC 9052](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9052.html).

For the present pilot, **LoRaWAN uplink and receipt downlink are the scarce resources for two-way traffic**; the FM programme slot limits centre-to-resident broadcast. The 1200-bit/s licensed voice-radio backhaul, if later authorized, is not the first byte-rate bottleneck at the reference load. These numbers do not establish a 15-minute private-exchange guarantee, and they rule out a high-volume chat service on the proposed bearers. Priority reports and short targeted instructions are the viable workload.

## What each bearer can actually carry

| Path | Capacity mechanism | Planning consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Centre → FM transmitter → radios/phones   | One scheduled audio feed, received by everyone in range at the same time; no return path | One encryption object can reach arbitrarily many *eligible* group recipients without extra broadcast time; unique messages and multiple individual key wraps spend the finite audio slot. A phone needs the offline app listening to a nearby FM receiver/speaker, or it can collect cached ciphertext at a station. Broadcast does not push to an unattended phone. |
| Phone → community station | Local Wi-Fi is the routine fast path; phone-speaker → station-mic acoustic modem is the independent fallback | One 128-byte object takes two 112-byte audio frames, two passes and ~5.89 s including the single 0.5 s lead in the current software waveform. Human walk-up, phone permissions and noise can dominate this ideal byte rate. |
| Station ↔ centre over private EU868 LoRaWAN | Small shared packets, rate and legal duty dependent, one gateway normally deaf while transmitting | Send compact encrypted reports up and small signed receipts down. Do not use it as a general message stream or to mirror 1 KB FM snapshots. Adding default frequencies in the same 1% sub-band helps collision distribution; it does **not** multiply each device's sub-band duty budget. [EU868 payload/rates](https://www.thethingsnetwork.org/docs/lorawan/regional-parameters/eu868/), [duty rule](https://www.thethingsnetwork.org/docs/lorawan/duty-cycle/), [gateway half-duplex](https://www.thethingsnetwork.org/docs/lorawan/limitations/). |
| Optional centre ↔ remote hub over licensed VHF/UHF | A separate operator-assigned two-way channel and packet modem; 1200-bit/s AFSK is the first comparison | Illustrative throughput is tens of kB/hour at a modest shared-channel allocation, but licence, voice priority, repeater occupancy, audio processing and RF loss determine the real budget. FM broadcast itself cannot return resident reports. [Dire Wolf modem profile](https://github.com/wb2osz/direwolf/blob/master/conf/generic.conf), [Latvian authorization route](https://www.esakari.lv/en/node/2098643). |

### FM audio slot: measured waveform time, hypothetical broadcaster allocation

The existing software fixture uses 128-byte modem frames with **112 application-object bytes**, a 0.5 s lead, a 0.3 s inter-burst gap and two passes. Its 378-byte signed public object takes four frames/pass and its generated WAV is **11.284 s**; the 314-byte private report is three frames/pass and **8.588 s**. Subtracting those fixture durations yields **1.348 s per transmitted frame including gap**. This is a file duration, not an off-air success rate. The `M0` waveform is the baseline; Rattlegram is a candidate to measure at the broadcaster, not an assumed gain. [Local fixture results](atbalsts-radio-security-proof/results.json), [Rattlegram implementation](https://github.com/aicodix/rattlegram).

Assume the broadcaster grants **one exclusive data-audio slot every five minutes** and the centre places one 378-byte signed public object in every slot. A proposed 128-byte fixed HPKE sealed private note plus an outer COSE/Ed25519 centre signature is **209 bytes** by the existing CBOR size rules, so it needs **two audio frames per pass**. Its 70-byte padded plaintext budget must contain the message ID, expiry, area and text; the size fixture accommodates at most 40 UTF-8 note bytes. Latvian accented letters can take multiple bytes, so a preinstalled phrase/area registry is valuable for practical instructions. This is a terse instruction, not a letter. The new private object is a size model, not implemented.

| Data-audio slot every 5 min | Unique 112-byte frames available with two passes | Frames used by 378-byte public object | Additional 209-byte private notes | Notes/hour, ideal |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 15 s | 5 | 4 | 0 | 0 |
| **30 s** | **10** | **4** | **3** | **36** |
| 60 s | 22 | 4 | 9 | 108 |

Each line assumes one combined scheduled transmission with one lead, no extra voice announcement, guard time or off-air loss. At 30 s, a full **1,024-byte** public snapshot consumes all ten unique frames and leaves no room for private notes in that cycle. A missed or late-tuned FM slot means a five-minute wait or longer; an individual recipient's delivery cannot be inferred merely from transmission. Reserving actual voice time, third repetitions or error-recovery slots lowers these counts. **Thirty seconds every five minutes occupies six minutes, or 10%, of a programme hour.** That is a substantial broadcaster commitment. The 30 s/5 min allocation is a **negotiation target**, not an existing entitlement; if only 15 seconds is granted, this public-plus-private mix has no private capacity. Routine private collection over station Wi-Fi is more practical where residents can reach a station.

### LoRaWAN: airtime and useful report rate

The table uses 125 kHz, coding rate 4/5, eight-symbol preamble, no optional MAC bytes, an 8-byte application fragment header and **one 1% uplink sub-band** (36 transmission seconds per hour *per station*). It includes LoRaWAN PHY overhead but no loss, retry, join, channel congestion or scheduling delay. The half-budget column intentionally reserves half the legal airtime for retries and control; it is an engineering planning rule, not a regulatory limit. Actual regional maximum application payload is 51/115/222 bytes at DR0/DR3/DR5 before MAC options. [EU868 parameters](https://www.thethingsnetwork.org/docs/lorawan/regional-parameters/eu868/).

| Object from one station | Rate | Frames | TX airtime/object | Pure airtime ceiling/hour | Half-budget planning/hour |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 128-byte encrypted report | DR0/SF12 | 3 | 8.38 s | 4 | 2 |
| 128-byte encrypted report | **DR3/SF9** | **2** | **0.965 s** | **37** | **18** |
| 128-byte encrypted report | DR5/SF7 | 1 | 0.246 s | 146 | 73 |
| 314-byte richer encrypted report | DR3/SF9 | 3 | 2.010 s | 17 | 8 |
| 209-byte signed/private object | DR3/SF9 | 2 | 1.333 s | 27 | 13 |

The pilot's **10 compact reports/hour per station** use **9.65 s/hour**, or **26.8%** of that station's 1% duty budget at DR3. That leaves 26.35 s/hour for retries, joins, status and any private replies. At DR0, ten reports/hour from one station would require **83.8 s/hour**, exceeding its 36 s budget even before retries. Thus sustained **DR3 in both directions at both core sites is the first go/no-go gate** for the proposed rate. More default 868.1/868.3/868.5 MHz channels do not evade this one-sub-band calculation. Different legal sub-bands could change the budget after a frequency-plan and hardware review; they are not an assumed multiplier. [Duty-cycle explanation](https://www.thethingsnetwork.org/docs/lorawan/duty-cycle/).

**Gateway downlink is a separate shared budget.** On the reference 869.525 MHz RX2 path at DR3, each **proposed** 105-byte signed centre receipt fits one downlink and takes **0.636 s**. Twenty receipts/hour use **12.72 s/hour** of transmitter time. The applicable Latvian 869.4–869.65 MHz row allows an alternative ≤10% duty-cycle path, or 360 s/hour in principle, under its specified conditions; every gateway transmission also makes the gateway temporarily unable to hear uplinks. As a conservative *design guardrail*, limit gateway transmission to **36 s/hour total** (1% wall-clock deaf time) until a real collision/reliability test justifies more. That leaves **23.28 s/hour**, enough for only **17** additional 209-byte private downlinks/hour at DR3 (1.333 s each) before retries. At default DR0/SF12, twenty 105-byte receipts alone use **151 s/hour**, about **4.2%** gateway deaf time and **42%** of that 10%-sub-band legal budget. The DR3 RX2 change has to be acknowledged by every powered station and validated in both directions; it cannot be assumed at the cell edge. [Latvian frequency plan, row 54](https://likumi.lv/ta/id/338729), [gateway limitation](https://www.thethingsnetwork.org/docs/lorawan/limitations/), [Class C scheduling conflicts](https://www.thethingsindustries.com/docs/hardware/devices/configuring-devices/class-c/).

At scale, separate per-device duty budgets do **not** mean unlimited aggregate throughput. For illustration only, if every station sends ten DR3 two-fragment reports/hour randomly across three equally loaded channels, a simple same-rate unslotted-ALOHA model gives a no-collision probability for *both fragments* of about **99.3% at two stations, 98.2% at five, 96.5% at ten and 93.1% at twenty**. It ignores capture, nonorthogonal spreading factors, interference, retransmission and the gateway's downlinks; it is a warning that collision and receipt load must be measured, not a predicted field success rate. Ten receipts per station use ~6.36 gateway TX seconds/hour, so the illustrative 36 s/hour downlink guardrail is nearly exhausted by **five** stations before private downlinks. A second active gateway with independently surveyed reception helps, but does not remove duty limits from each transmitter.

### Optional licensed VHF/UHF packet backhaul

[Dire Wolf's standard voice-radio modem](https://github.com/wb2osz/direwolf/blob/master/conf/generic.conf) gives a **1200-bit/s AFSK** starting point: raw channel rate 150 bytes/s while keyed. For a concrete estimate, add 20 bytes framing, 5% bit stuffing and 0.3 s PTT/preamble/turnaround, and allow **10% of one shared channel's hour** to data after voice priority. A 128-byte object then takes ~1.34 s keyed, yielding ~**34 kB/hour**; a 209-byte object takes ~1.90 s, yielding ~**40 kB/hour**. These are *assumed allocation* examples, not permitted airtime or measured goodput. Retries, a repeater, user traffic and one shared half-duplex channel can cut them sharply. Use an individually authorized service and get the operator's conditions before choosing modulation, encryption or frequency. Amateur radio is not a defensible default for confidential resident traffic; the [ITU amateur-service rule](https://search.itu.int/history/HistoryDigitalCollectionDocLibrary/1.49.48.en.101.pdf) restricts transmissions encoded to obscure meaning between countries. Latvian domestic rules and any specific licence require separate review.

## Private channel design and its byte cost

1. **Enrol before the outage.** Each user device generates its own X25519 encryption key pair; the private key stays on that device in protected storage. The centre records the verified person/device ↔ public-key binding, expiry, allowed area/channel and key epoch. Provision the centre's Ed25519 verification key in the offline app. Register secondary devices as separate recipients. A public key by itself does not prove who owns it; verify it at enrolment (for example an in-person QR exchange with authenticated staff). Never send a private key or shared group secret as plaintext over FM, LoRaWAN or a volunteer bridge.
2. **One person: 209-byte pilot envelope.** Construct a fixed **128-byte HPKE Base** object using the existing X25519/HKDF-SHA256/AES-128-GCM suite and a separate 10-byte header: ASCII `ATP1` (4), fresh random value (4), plaintext-pad length `0x0046` (2). Use domain-separated `info = "atbalsts/private/v1" || 0x00 || header`. The 70-byte padded plaintext starts with a two-byte length and deterministic CBOR `[1, messageId, expiresAt, areaId, recipientKeyEpoch, note]`: unsigned 32-bit IDs/times, at most 40 UTF-8 note bytes and zero padding. The maximum CBOR body is 64 bytes, so the length plus body fits the pad. Sign the *exact 128 encrypted bytes* with the centre's COSE_Sign1/Ed25519 key, yielding **209 bytes** with the same four-byte **centre signing** `kid` as the size fixture. Relays cache or rebroadcast exact bytes, never decrypt. The app verifies signature, attempts HPKE opening with its current and bounded retained keys, checks expiry/replay and rejects invalid CBOR/pad. Omit a stable cleartext user ID from the FM header; at pilot volumes a device can attempt opening each private envelope. This keeps recipient identity out of the broadcast object, although the centre's own directory still knows the route. The 209-byte result is a **framing size check only**; this private-message schema and implementation need test vectors and security review. [HPKE](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9180.html), [COSE](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9052.html).
3. **One instruction to several distinct user keys:** encrypt the content once with a random content key, then HPKE-wrap that key separately to each recipient device and sign the *whole bundle*. In an illustrative 64-byte-padded-content binary layout, a complete object is **606 bytes for five recipient devices** and **1,026 bytes for ten**. One 30 s FM slot that also carries a 378-byte public object can carry the five-recipient bundle (six private frames); it cannot carry the ten-recipient bundle (ten private frames) in the same cycle. The centre must pad content and sign the recipient-wrap list; recipient hints can be omitted for privacy at the cost of more trial decryptions. Exact binary encoding and interoperability are future work.
4. **A team group:** encrypt once to a group content key and sign as the centre. The same illustrative 64-byte-padded-content object is ~**185 bytes**, regardless of audience count, so group delivery is efficient. It is private *from outsiders*, but **every current holder of the group key can read it**. A removed member can still read future traffic until a new secret reaches everyone else; no rotation can make previously recorded traffic secret again. Pre-provision small, stable team groups for the first pilot and distribute replacement keys individually when membership changes. [MLS RFC 9420](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9420.html) is the stronger future group-key framework for dynamic groups, but its ordered commits/welcome traffic and missed-update recovery need a measured offline delivery design. LoRaWAN multicast can reduce duplicate *station* downlinks when the identical encrypted object must reach several station boxes, but its shared radio key does not give each resident a private phone key or increase one transmission's airtime budget. [LoRa multicast setup](https://lora-alliance.org/resource_hub/lorawan-remote-multicast-setup-specification-v1-0-0/).
5. **Resident → centre, and true person-to-person.** The existing report pattern already seals plaintext on the phone to the centre's public key before any volunteer station or LoRa gateway sees it. For a message that even the centre may not read, the sender must encrypt to the other resident's verified public key and authenticate their authorship inside that encrypted object; the centre only routes opaque bytes and cannot inspect or moderate its plaintext. A situational agent that must review content can do so only if an authorized party discloses it or the agent is itself a recipient. This is a governance choice, not a radio or cryptographic shortcut. [LoRaWAN's separate device→application-server protection](https://lora-alliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/lorawantm_specification_-v1.1.pdf) does not protect phone plaintext from the station unless application encryption begins on the phone.

**What “only the user can decrypt” really means:** outsiders without the relevant private key should find decryption computationally infeasible under standard assumptions and correct implementation; it is not absolute impossibility. The sender knows what it wrote. A stolen/unlocked phone, substituted enrolment key, compromised centre, malicious app, exposed backup or group-key leak can reveal content. [HPKE explicitly lacks forward secrecy against later recipient-key compromise and supplies no application-level replay prevention](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9180.html#section-9.7); a recorder could later decrypt old FM traffic if a long-lived user key is stolen. Rotate keys, use unique message IDs/epochs, expire and deduplicate objects, keep old keys only for a bounded delivery window, and use a reviewed ratcheting protocol if protection of past recorded traffic after device compromise becomes a requirement. When a phone clock is uncertain after an outage, do not silently treat an old decrypted instruction as current; retain the highest verified centre epoch/sequence and display freshness as uncertain until time is established. Fixed padding masks short-message length but radio time, sender timing, area and membership hints can still leak metadata. The current browser proof demonstrates formats, not hardened on-device private-key custody; production user keys need a reviewed native/offline storage and recovery design. [Current proof limitations](atbalsts-radio-security-proof/protocol-spec.md).

## Admission control and a viable first service promise

- Keep public spoken emergency guidance and a small signed digital public object **first** in each agreed FM slot. Allocate private ciphertext a measured remainder; do not let private traffic suppress the accessible voice notice.
- Accept priority short reports and targeted instructions into a durable queue. At the station, reserve a per-device LoRa airtime ledger and a separate gateway downlink ledger; meter airtime rather than counting bytes alone. Reject or visibly defer intake when an acknowledged queue or airtime reservation is full. Never say “recipient read it” when only FM transmission or station storage is known.
- Distinguish **queued at station**, **committed at centre**, **broadcast on FM**, **app decrypted**, and **recipient acknowledged**. FM provides no inherent read receipt. A phone acknowledgment must return through a station and consumes LoRa uplink. If all 36 theoretical 30-second-slot private deliveries/hour also demand separate 128-byte acknowledgments, splitting them evenly between two stations adds ~17.36 LoRa TX seconds/hour per station; together with ten reports/hour, each station would use ~27.0 of its 36 legal seconds before retries. Batch acknowledgments only with a verified, nonforgeable phone-to-centre format, or treat them as best-effort delayed receipts. A station may make *all* cached private ciphertext available over its offline Wi-Fi; each app filters/decrypts locally, so the station need not know which resident message a phone took.
- Set the first fundable pilot to **two DR3-surveyed stations, ten 128-byte reports/hour each, twenty signed centre receipts/hour, one 378-byte public object every five minutes, and no more than three 209-byte centre-to-user private notes in an agreed 30-second FM slot**. Measure actual off-air complete-object delivery and a legal 72-hour blackout run. Add a *separate* trial of 209-byte private downlinks through LoRa only if the gateway's receipt, deaf-time and survey headroom allow it. For larger communities, load-test the network with the expected mix and add gateway diversity or a higher-capacity authorized backhaul before increasing the advertised message rate.

The decisive evidence is not a bit-rate benchmark. It is a timed end-to-end exercise logging **object bytes, scheduled slot, radio data rate, sub-band TX seconds, gateway TX/RX conflicts, complete-object success, key epoch, queue delay and recipient receipt state**. The [field log template](atbalsts-radio-field-trial-template.csv) has columns for these checks; keep actual recipient identities and plaintext out of the measurement export.
