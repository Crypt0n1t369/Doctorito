Business case Cicada

A data channel made of sound

Bytes go into a speaker; every device in earshot gets them back. No pairing, no network, no camera. The technology works. The question this answers is who pays for it, and why the obvious way to sell it fails the buyer who needs it most.

What is being sold

Ten times faster than the open-source option, and it works below the noise floor

Cicada modulates arbitrary bytes into audio between 671.9 and 2312.5 Hz — FFT bins 43 to 148 at an 8 kHz sample rate — and demodulates them back on anything with a microphone. OFDM across 106 carriers, differential QPSK, a rate-½ K=7 convolutional code, soft-decision Viterbi, and a 128 ms chirp preamble found by matched filter.

Delivery rate against channel signal-to-noise

128-byte frames decode 97 times in 100 at −4 dB — with the noise carrying more power than the signal — then fall off a cliff by −5.5 dB. That margin is what makes an audible band usable at a polite volume. Additive white noise, 60 trials per point; reproduce with npm run bench.

Three properties fall out of the physics, and each is awkward to buy elsewhere. No pairing — Bluetooth needs discovery and a session, NFC needs a tap, QR needs a camera and a person who knows to point it; sound needs a device that is already listening. One-to-many at zero marginal cost — a push notification to 500 phones is 500 messages, a Cicada broadcast is one transmission. Range is the room — sound stops at the walls, so proximity is a property of the medium rather than something you attest to with GPS that can be spoofed.

And it travels wherever audio already travels: a PA system, a phone call, a radio broadcast, a video file, a voicemail. That is a distribution network nobody has to build.

Who buys

The buyer with the sharpest need cannot use your cloud

A · Developers

Self-serve

Small teams adding an offline handoff, a proximity check, a device-provisioning path. They arrive through documentation and leave through documentation. This is not where the revenue is — it is where the evidence is.

$0 – $49 / month
B · Operators

Mid-market

Venues, transit, museums, retail, broadcasters. They already have speakers everywhere and a content system driving them. They buy because the alternative — an app install, a network join, a QR sticker somebody maintains — has a conversion rate they can measure and hate.

$300 – $2,000 / month
C · Regulated

Air-gapped

Emergency management, defence, industrial control, healthcare. The sharpest need and the most money — and it cannot call your HTTPS endpoint. An organisation choosing a medium because it works without a network will not render its audio over one.

$25,000 – $150,000 / year
This is the trap the obvious business model walks into. If SaaS is the only way to buy, the buyer with the strongest need is the one who cannot. The correct response is not to talk them into the cloud — it is to sell them an annual, per-site, self-hosted licence with source escrow and support.

So the model is deliberately dual. The SaaS is the funnel, the documentation and the proof. The on-premises licence is the revenue. Each makes the other credible: a self-hosted buyer will not take a black box seriously, and a developer will not adopt something with no visible production users.

Pro's ceiling is calibrated: 50,000 renders is one venue broadcasting a fresh message every 30 seconds, twelve hours a day. A single busy site fits; a second one does not.

Receiving is free permanently, and not out of generosity

Decoding happens on the listener's own device. It costs nothing, cannot be observed, and any attempt to meter it would be a lie enforced by a licence agreement. Making it free is the honest description of what is happening — and it is the entire adoption mechanism. Once an estate of devices is listening, somebody has to pay for something to say.

Renders are the wrong meter for half the market

A museum with twenty exhibits renders twenty transmissions and plays them for a year. Metering renders would leave that customer in the free tier forever while running a real deployment. So the plans meter two different things on purpose: channels and cards price deployment scale, and renders and decodes price dynamic content. A customer pays on whichever axis they actually consume.

That is why the Free tier's binding constraint is one channel, not a thousand renders. One channel is a demo. The second channel is a deployment.

The defensible part

Airtime that stops depending on payload size

Airtime is the scarce resource. Storage is not. Publish a payload once through the API, broadcast its four-byte code, and receivers resolve it from a bundle cached over their normal network connection — so resolution works offline too.

On a small payload the saving is unremarkable, and saying so is cheaper than being caught overselling it. The argument is the last three rows: beyond a kilobyte, sending directly stops being a product at all.

The defensible position is not the modem — it is standard OFDM and anyone competent can write one. It is the card registry, key custody and profile tooling: the parts that turn a modem into something you can operate, and the parts that take a year of arguing with real rooms to get right.

Competition, honestly

ggwave Open source, free, 8–16 B/s. If someone's payload is ten bytes once a minute they should use it, and saying so is cheaper than losing the argument later.
LISNR Ultrasonic, enterprise-only, closed. Inaudible carriers are a covert side channel; Cicada is audible on purpose. An easier compliance conversation.
Chirp Acquired by Sonos in 2020 and withdrawn. Their customers had to migrate. A live, addressable list.
QR codes Free and universal, and genuinely better when a person will point a camera. Worse for hands-free, one-to-many, headless devices, anything moving.
BLE beacons Better range and battery. Needs hardware in the room and OS permissions that keep tightening. Cicada needs a speaker that is already there.
What would have to be true

Five risks, each with the test that settles it

The simulations must survive real rooms

Every number in this document comes from software. No physical speaker, room or microphone appears anywhere in the measurement.

Test · a measured matrix across 3 phones, 2 speakers, 4 real spaces — published with the failures included

The reverberation limit is real and unfixed

The cyclic prefix is 8 ms. Rooms whose echoes run much longer smear one symbol into the next, and volume does not help. An atrium is a harder problem than distance ever was.

Test · if reverberant halls prove unusable rather than merely hard, segment B shrinks to small rooms

Somebody's audio pipeline will eat it

OFDM has a high peak-to-average ratio, so a broadcast limiter pushed hard flattens exactly the peaks carrying the information. Voice-tuned noise suppression is built to remove things that are not speech, and a modem in the speech band looks like something to remove.

Test · already visible in simulation — micro frames drop to 35% through a modelled broadcast chain

Audibility is a product constraint, not only a virtue

You cannot transmit continuously in a quiet space without annoying people. Every deployment is bursts, and that shapes what can be sold.

Test · sound-level and duty-cycle limits agreed with a pilot venue before the contract, not after

The on-prem segment has a long sales cycle

Twelve to eighteen months, security review, procurement. The SaaS has to fund the wait.

Test · first security questionnaire returned inside 90 days of first contact
Where to start

In this order, because each step is what makes the next one sayable

  • Measure real rooms and publish it, failures included. Nothing else can be claimed honestly until this exists, and it is the asset competitors will not bother to make.
  • Ship the receiver SDK free and open. It is the wedge, and it cannot be metered anyway.
  • Win one lighthouse in each of B and C. A transit operator or museum, and one emergency-management or industrial buyer. The second one pays for the year.
  • Sell the card registry, not the modem. The modem is table stakes. Airtime that does not depend on payload size is the thing nobody else has bothered to build.

Measured — throughput per profile; SNR cliffs in additive white noise; delivery through eight modelled acoustic conditions; bit-exact round trips at 8, 16, 22.05, 44.1 and 48 kHz; signature verification, tamper and forgery rejection; duplicate suppression; reassembly under frame loss and reordering. 92 tests.

Not measured — any physical speaker, room or microphone. No RF, no phone hardware, no real broadcast chain, no load testing, no security audit. The DSP core derives from the Atbalsts radio-validation work, where it was verified bit-exact against an independent Python implementation; that provenance is why it is trustworthy enough to build on, and it is not a substitute for the field measurement above.
