Strategy note Cicada Companion to the business case

Why Cicada stays audible

Two questions worth settling with measurements rather than opinion: should the modem move into the near-ultrasonic band, and is there already a way to do this over radio without sound at all? The answers are no and yes — and the second one narrows the product to something much more defensible than “data over radio”.

Question one

Near-ultrasonic costs 31 dB and buys no speed

The appeal is obvious: move the carrier to 19 kHz and the data stops being audible. To test it rather than argue it, the existing modem was upconverted to arbitrary centre frequencies, run through models of a phone loudspeaker and MEMS microphone, atmospheric absorption, room reverberation and each pipeline's cutoff, then decoded back. A decode either happens or it does not.

Where each band dies — cutoffs of the paths audio actually travels

FM's 15 kHz limit is not a codec quirk but the standard itself: programme audio stops there so the 19 kHz stereo pilot tone has room. A near-ultrasonic carrier and the FM pilot are competing for the same frequency.

Not degraded — absent. The audio plays, the receiver hears nothing, and the failure looks exactly like a decoder bug. This is the most expensive mistake available in data-over-sound, which is why the service now takes the delivery path as an input.

And the range collapses

A phone loudspeaker and a MEMS microphone are both flat through the voice band and both fall away above about 10 kHz. End to end the pair is 31.5 dB down at 19 kHz. The modem cliffs at −4 dB SNR, so that penalty comes straight off the range.

Spherical spreading plus atmospheric absorption, which is itself forty times worse at 19 kHz than at 1.5 kHz. Ultrasound is an arm's-length medium on consumer hardware, not a room-scale one.

Two things people expect that are not true

It is not faster. Throughput is set by occupied bandwidth, not by how high the carrier sits. The modem occupies 1.64 kHz wherever you put it, and 18.0–19.6 kHz is about all the room there is before phone speakers stop reproducing anything at all. Same bandwidth, same 86 B/s.

It is not inaudible. Under-18s hear a 19 kHz tone plainly and many people under 25 hear it at close range — which is precisely the range where it works. Dogs hear to about 45 kHz and cats to 64 kHz, so 19 kHz is comfortably inside both.

There is also a reputational cost. Inaudible beacons are how ultrasonic cross-device tracking worked, and that technique drew regulatory attention and app-store bans. An audible channel is one you can point at and say: when this device transmits, you can hear it. That is a materially easier conversation with a procurement officer, a works council, or a data-protection regulator — and it is the harder thing to claim back once given up.

The one place ultrasound genuinely wins

Reverberation is the modem's real enemy: the cyclic prefix is 8 ms and a longer echo smears one symbol into the next. Air absorbs high frequencies faster, so a room with a 0.4 s decay at 1 kHz has only about 0.18 s at 19 kHz. Setting the link-budget penalty aside to isolate the effect, at that reverberation 19 kHz delivered 100% where the audible band managed 33%.

That is real, and it roughly doubles the reverberation the modem tolerates. It is also worth little in practice, because it only applies inside the one metre where ultrasound works at all — and at one metre the direct sound dominates the echo anyway.

Verdict: ultrasonic is a different product, not an upgrade. It is an arm's-length, direct-playback channel, which is a real but small niche already served by NFC and QR codes. Ship it as a selectable band for the pairing case. Do not build the business on it, and never let it become the default.
Question two

Yes, radio can already carry data — to receivers that are not phones

This is the sharper question, and the answer reframes the product. FM has carried a data channel since the 1980s: RDS, on a 57 kHz subcarrier, at 1187.5 bit/s. That is not merely comparable to Cicada — it is the same speed.

The argument for Cicada was never throughput. RDS is exactly as fast. The argument is entirely about which receiver you can reach.

And that is where RDS stops. Phones cannot receive it. Apple has shipped no FM receiver since the iPhone 7 in 2016 and never connected the circuitry in the models before it. Most Android flagships dropped the chip, carriers asked manufacturers to disable what remained, and the headphone jack that served as the FM antenna went with it.

The channel that does reach every phone is cell broadcast — EU-Alert, mandated across member states since June 2022. It is unaffected by network congestion, it needs no app, and for public warning it is the correct primary channel. It also requires a working mobile network and a working grid.

Transmitter → receiver

Cell broadcast

Reaches every phone in a cell, instantly, with no app and no congestion penalty.

Needs the mobile network and the grid to be working.

Speaker → microphone

Cicada

Reaches every phone in earshot of any speaker — a radio, a PA, a laptop, a phone call.

Needs a speaker in earshot. Needs nothing else.

So the positioning is not “data over radio”. It is the last hop. Cicada is the only one of the three that spans the gap between broadcast infrastructure that keeps running without a network — FM transmitters on generators, station PA systems, a phone on speaker — and the receiver every person already carries. RDS owns the first hop and cannot finish it. Cell broadcast finishes it beautifully, until the network is the thing that failed.

That is a narrower claim than the one worth starting with, and a much harder one to attack. It also settles the ultrasonic question a second time: the whole point is that a normal radio loudspeaker in a normal room can deliver it, and a normal radio loudspeaker cannot reproduce 19 kHz — even if FM were willing to carry it, which it is not.

Where the money is

Four niches, ranked by how quickly someone writes a cheque

1

Accessible passenger information

€2–8k per site per year

A spoken platform announcement is useless to a deaf passenger and to anyone who does not speak the language. The current answer is an induction loop: expensive to install, reaches only hearing aids with a T-coil, and carries no text. Put the same announcement in the PA audio as data and every phone in the concourse can show it as text, in the reader's own language, with no network and no app-store dependency — over speakers that are already installed.

Driver · European Accessibility Act in force since 28 June 2025, transition to 2030, penalties to €100k or 4% of turnover. Sectoral rail rules (TSI PRM) already govern visual and audible passenger information — confirm the precise obligation per operator and country.
Against · nothing competes directly. Loops solve a narrower problem and cost more.
2

Resilient public warning

€150–500k per country

Civil protection agencies already have cell broadcast and it is the right primary channel. Cicada is the documented second path for when the grid or the network is the thing that failed: FM stays on generators, people still have radios and phones, and the phone cannot hear RDS. Sell it as the fallback that gets exercised in drills, never as a replacement — a replacement pitch loses to a mandated incumbent and deserves to.

Driver · Critical Entities Resilience Directive and NIS2 both push named, tested fallbacks for essential services.
Against · cell broadcast, which wins whenever the network is up. Say so first.
3

Air-gapped data ingress

€25–150k per site per year

Getting configuration, attestations or key material into a machine with no network is done today with USB sticks, which are a notorious attack vector. An acoustic path is one-way by construction, cannot exfiltrate without a speaker, and is audible — so it is auditable by anyone standing in the room.

Against · many security teams classify any acoustic channel as a threat to be blocked. The counter-argument is that a sanctioned, audible, logged channel is better than an unmonitored one, but expect to make it more than once.
4

Arm's-length device provisioning

per-device royalty

Commissioning headless sensors and controllers that have a microphone but no screen, no keypad and no network. This is the one place near-ultrasonic earns its keep, because the path is direct playback at under a metre — exactly the envelope the measurements leave it.

Against · NFC and QR are cheaper and better whenever the device has a screen or a person has a camera. Needs an embedded C port before it is sellable.
What changes in the model

The compliance buyer procures sites, not API calls

The original plan metered renders and decodes, with channels and cards pricing deployment scale. That is right for developers and wrong for the buyer who now looks most attractive. A rail operator does not think in API calls. It thinks in stations, and it buys an annual line item per station that renews without anyone revisiting the decision.

Add a site licence between Scale and Self-hosted

  • Per site, per year — a station, a terminal, a venue. Unmetered rendering within it, because the content is mostly static and metering it would price the product wrongly for the people most likely to buy.
  • Runs on their hardware. Station PA systems are not internet-connected and should not become so for this. The self-hosted build already exists.
  • Priced against the alternative, which is an induction loop install at five figures per platform, not against an API call.

A hundred-station operator is €200–800k a year on that basis, renewing, from one sale. That is a different business from a $49 subscription, and the SaaS tiers keep earning their place as the thing developers find first.

Two product decisions that follow directly

  • The delivery path is now an input, not an assumption. The service asks where the audio is going and refuses to be optimistic about a band that path removes. This is shipped: GET /v1/paths, and /v1/capacity?path=fmBroadcast&band=nearUltrasonic returns a warning and the band to use instead.
  • Near-ultrasonic ships as a band, gated to direct playback. Selectable where it works, refused where it does not, never the default.
Next

What would settle these

  • Measure one real PA system. The 18 kHz figure for venue PA is the least certain number here and the one the best niche depends on. A single afternoon in a real concourse with a measurement microphone settles it.
  • Get the accessibility obligation in writing. Whether visual duplication of live announcements is required, or merely expected, decides whether niche one is a compliance purchase or a nice-to-have. That is a question for a transport regulator, not an engineer.
  • Confirm the transducer curve on three real phones. The 31.5 dB penalty is modelled. If real hardware is better than modelled — some phones are — the ultrasonic envelope grows from one metre to perhaps three, which does not change the verdict but does change niche four.
