# Precedents: attempts at support-powered, mission-owning convergence

23 September 2026. Three research agents (technical, ideological, social); each claim sourced and labelled primary / independent / marketing. Raw output, not edited.

################################################################################

## Wikipedia (2001–present)
TRIED: Converge millions of anonymous edits into one trusted encyclopedia using neutrality, verifiability and consensus norms.
PROVED: Open contribution can produce a durable, widely trusted body of knowledge; it remains the reference case of legitimate convergence.
STALLED: Active editors (5+ edits/month) stopped growing in early 2007 and declined steadily; the cause was falling newcomer retention, driven by reverts and tools built for quality control. Human pageviews then fell 8% (Mar–Aug 2025 vs 2024) as AI summaries answer instead, and the foundation warned that fewer visits mean fewer volunteers and donors.
LESSON: The check that protects the record must not reject newcomers' first contributions, and consumers such as AI answer engines must route attention back, or the support loop dies.
SRC: <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0002764212469365>[independent] <https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Research:The_Rise_and_Decline>[primary] <https://www.techcrunch.com/2025/10/18/wikipedia-says-traffic-is-falling-due-to-ai-search-summaries-and-social-video>[independent]

## Open-source maintenance (xz utils case) (xz incident 2021–2024; the pattern is ongoing)
TRIED: Converge many contributors into shared infrastructure, with a maintainer who merges and foundations behind them.
PROVED: It works where checking is cheap (code, CI). But a vendor survey of 400+ maintainers found 60% unpaid and 60% had quit or considered quitting (2024).
STALLED: xz was kept alive by one unpaid, overstretched maintainer. Sock-puppet accounts pressured him to hand maintainership to a 'helpful' contributor, who earned commit rights over years and inserted a backdoor in Feb–Mar 2024.
LESSON: Who may accept contributions is the attack surface. Pay for and spread the curator role, and treat pressure to hand over trust as a capture signal.
SRC: <https://4008838.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/4008838/2024-tidelift-state-of-the-open-source-maintainer-report.pdf>[marketing] <https://securelist.com/xz-backdoor-story-part-2-social-engineering/112476/>[independent] <https://www.darkreading.com/application-security/attacker-social-engineered-backdoor-code-into-xz-utils>[independent]

## OpenStreetMap (2004–present)
TRIED: Build a volunteer-made, openly licensed map of the world.
PROVED: It became infrastructure important enough that Apple, Microsoft and Facebook hired editors: more than 17 million paid edits in five years (to 2019).
STALLED: Where corporate teams work, non-corporate editors' share of road editing fell to under 25%, from about 70% in 2017, and there were tensions over the community splitting.
LESSON: Success attracts well-funded contributors who can dominate volume; show who contributed what, and keep governance separate from contribution volume.
SRC: <https://www.mdpi.com/2220-9964/8/5/232>[independent] <https://joemorrison.medium.com/openstreetmap-is-having-a-moment-dcc7eef1bb01>[independent]

## MIT Climate CoLab (2009–2022 at MIT; then moved to Wazoku/InnoCentive)
TRIED: Crowdsource climate plans through contests judged by experts and the community.
PROVED: Scale of participation: more than 125,000 members, over 100 contests and over 150 selected solutions (its own figures).
STALLED: No published measure of proposals implemented (unknown). In 2022 it was spun out into a commercial challenge platform.
LESSON: Contests produce ranked proposals, not progress; without an owner who takes up and carries out the winners, convergence ends at a prize list.
SRC: <https://cci.mit.edu/climatecolab/>[primary] <https://mitsloan.mit.edu/press/mit-center-collective-intelligences-climate-colab-spins-out-and-migrates-to-wazokus-global-challenge-community-innocentive>[primary]

## Polymath Project (2009–end unknown (dormant))
TRIED: Solve open math problems in the open, using blogs and a wiki, led by star hosts (Gowers, Tao).
PROVED: Polymath1 found a new proof. Of 16 projects, 6 reached peer-reviewed publication. The top 10% of contributors wrote 80% of posts, and occasional contributors measurably boosted the core's productivity.
STALLED: 10 of 16 projects never published. Progress depended on a few highly active hosts and a small committed core.
LESSON: Expect a 10/80 split: design for a small committed core plus a cheap-to-absorb periphery, not for mass equal participation.
SRC: <https://www.nature.com/articles/461879a>[primary] <https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2105.02303>[independent] <https://arxiv.org/pdf/1409.8361>[primary]

## vTaiwan / Pol.is (2014–present (volunteer-only since government support faded))
TRIED: Crowd deliberation on national regulation, with Pol.is mapping the points of agreement across opinion clusters.
PROVED: About 26 deliberations (2015–2018), roughly 80% of which led to some government action (its own count); the Uber case (2015) surfaced agreement across clusters.
STALLED: Its outputs were never binding. Political support faded after 2018, the state built its own portal (Join, 2019), COVID cut the in-person stage, and it now runs on volunteers.
LESSON: Legitimate convergence is wasted without a decision-taker committed in advance to respond; get that uptake path agreed before collecting input.
SRC: <https://www.designingopendemocracy.com/blog/2026/05/25/taiwans-digital-democracy-experiment-what-it-shows-what-it-doesnt/>[independent] <https://compdemocracy.org/case-studies/2014-vtaiwan/>[primary]

## Community Notes (X, formerly Birdwatch) (2021–present)
TRIED: Crowd fact-checks that are shown only when raters who usually disagree both rate them helpful (a 'bridging' algorithm).
PROVED: The bridging rule gives notes trust across partisan lines, and published notes reduce engagement with and spread of false posts.
STALLED: Only about 10% of notes reach 'helpful', and that share is falling. Having a note published causally raises future writing, so low throughput threatens the volunteer base. Uptake is uneven across languages.
LESSON: Bridging buys legitimacy at the cost of throughput; contributors must see their work land or they stop.
SRC: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.00650>[independent] <https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.13322>[independent] <https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.19947>[independent]

## LiquidFeedback in Germany's Pirate Party (2010–2013 (period studied))
TRIED: Liquid democracy: members vote directly or delegate their vote, on a standing online platform.
PROVED: 13,836 users, 499,009 votes and 6,517 initiatives. Delegation changed only 15.1% of outcomes, and the 38 'super-voters' mostly voted with the majority.
STALLED: Active use fell sharply about 180 days after the 2011 party congress as debate grew over super-voters' democratic legitimacy. Participation was concentrated: 54 members cast more than 1,000 votes each.
LESSON: Power that looks concentrated erodes legitimacy even when it is used benignly; make influence visible and bounded from day one.
SRC: <https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1503.07723>[independent] <https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/view/14618>[independent]

## Collective Constitutional AI (Anthropic + Collective Intelligence Project) (2023)
TRIED: About 1,000 roughly representative US adults wrote and voted on rules for an AI model via Polis; a model was trained on the resulting 'public constitution'.
PROVED: 1,002 participants, 1,127 statements and 38,252 votes produced a usable constitution with about 50% overlap with Anthropic's own, and more weight on objectivity, impartiality and accessibility.
STALLED: A one-off snapshot. No standing constituency or rerun was confirmed, and its influence on production models is unknown.
LESSON: A representative sample can produce legible principles cheaply, but a single event is not a durable mandate; build for repeated rounds.
SRC: <https://www.anthropic.com/research/collective-constitutional-ai-aligning-a-language-model-with-public-input>[primary] <https://facctconference.org/static/papers24/facct24-94.pdf>[primary]

## OpenAI Democratic Inputs to AI grants (2023–2024)
TRIED: $100,000 grants to 10 teams (from nearly 1,000 applicants) to prototype democratic processes for setting AI rules.
PROVED: Hybrid processes, with AI aggregating and people curating and casting a final vote, were seen as efficient and legitimate. 85% in one process backed updating the policy regularly as issues arise.
STALLED: Hard problems remained: recruiting across the digital divide, producing a coherent output from diverse views, and transparency. How far results were adopted into models is unknown.
LESSON: Let AI do the aggregation, but have people ratify; participants expect a living document, not a fixed verdict.
SRC: <https://openai.com/index/democratic-inputs-to-ai-grant-program-update/>[primary] <https://openai.com/index/democratic-inputs-to-ai/>[primary]

FAILURE MODES:
- Quality gates that protect the record repel newcomers: contributions that never land (Wikipedia reverts, Community Notes' 10% publication rate) make contributors leave.
- Convergence with no decision-taker committed in advance stays advisory: vTaiwan, Climate CoLab and one-off AI assemblies produced outputs that no one was bound to carry out.
- Dependence on one champion or a tiny core: Audrey Tang for vTaiwan, Gowers and Tao for Polymath, one maintainer for xz. When they tire or leave, the project stalls or is captured.
- Capture through volume or through earned trust: funded teams dominate OpenStreetMap edits in their areas, and a patient actor earned merge rights in xz.
- Visible concentration of power erodes legitimacy even when that power is used benignly (LiquidFeedback super-voters).
- Bridging or consensus rules buy trust at the cost of speed and coverage, which starves contributors of feedback (Community Notes).
- Downstream consumers take the output without returning attention or money, breaking the support loop: Wikipedia's human pageviews fell 8%, and 60% of maintainers are unpaid.
- One-off events yield snapshots, not a standing constituency with an ongoing mandate (Collective Constitutional AI, Democratic Inputs).
- Convergence reflects who showed up: uptake differs by language and across the digital divide, so the 'consensus' is partial.
- Ostrom's principle that those affected help make the rules is often missing: rules are set by the platform or founders, and contributors cannot change them (inferred across cases).

NEW NOW: Three things are genuinely cheaper in 2026: synthesis (LLMs can cluster and summarise thousands of contributions, as Democratic Inputs' hybrid processes showed), agreement-finding (bridging algorithms are proven at scale), and first-pass checking (cheap per-item triage). None of these was the binding constraint in the cases above. The stalls were social and institutional: no committed decision-taker, a thin core of curators, contributions that never landed, capture by volume or through earned trust, and a support loop drained by those who consume the output. AI makes two of these worse. It raises the volume curators must filter and makes xz-style trust-building cheaper to fake. It also drains the attention that funded commons (Wikipedia -8% human pageviews, 2025). The odds improve only if the portal does four things: (1) pre-commits an owner who is bound to respond to what converges; (2) uses AI to cut curator time per item rather than to add contributions; (3) shows every contributor, fast, what became of their input; (4) makes influence visible and bounded, and ties the growth of support (attention, money, compute) to outputs people can check. Without these, the historical pattern of early success followed by stalling or capture is likely to repeat.

## Online petition and campaign platforms (change.org; also Avaaz, MoveOn) (unknown start (pre-2010)–present; founding dates not re-checked)
TRIED: Turn one-click signatures into pressure on a named decision-maker, at very large scale.
PROVED: change.org says it has 116,180 confirmed victories in 196 countries (its own count). By its own data, 40% of winning petitions had fewer than 200 signatures, so a small, targeted ask can win.
STALLED: No overall win rate is published. People who showed support publicly were no more likely to donate or volunteer afterwards than a control group (JCR 2014). A 'write with AI' tool on change.org made 1.5M petitions read more alike but did not improve outcomes.
LESSON: Count outcomes against a named target, not signatures. Follow the first act of support straight away with a concrete ask. Better AI drafting does not move outcomes.
SRC: <https://www.change.org/impact>[marketing] <https://www.fastcompany.com/3022086/what-makes-for-a-winning-changeorg-petition>[independent] <https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/40/6/1149/2907521>[independent] <https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.13949>[independent]

## Occupy Wall Street general assemblies (2011–2012)
TRIED: Leaderless public assemblies deciding by modified consensus (about 90%, with blocks allowed), plus open working groups for food, media, legal and direct action.
PROVED: A crowd organised a working camp through many working groups within weeks, and other cities copied the assembly format.
STALLED: Critics said consensus-only, leaderless decision-making held it back, and it never agreed demands. By January 2012 the press reported infighting and 'insular and trivial' discussions.
LESSON: Open deliberation needs a legitimate way to decide, delegate and record commitments, or it breaks down as it grows and once the shared space is gone.
SRC: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_assembly_(Occupy_movement)>[independent] <https://edition.cnn.com/2011/10/18/us/occupy-wall-street-consensus-building>[independent] <https://macc.nyc/img/assemblies/OWSStructure.pdf>[primary]

## Occupy Sandy (2012–unknown)
TRIED: Reuse Occupy's networks to send volunteers and supplies to hurricane survivors, organising horizontally over social media.
PROVED: About 60,000 volunteers at its peak, more than four times the number the Red Cross deployed. A DHS study named its horizontal structure and social-media mobilisation as success drivers.
STALLED: Unpaid volunteers had to go back to paid work or ask for stipends. Burnout followed, and organisers called the 'extreme horizontalism' problematic. Afterwards it continued only in pockets, such as cooperatives in the Rockaways.
LESSON: Concrete local needs with visible results turn goodwill into action fast. Keeping it going after the surge needs a funded core and a clear handoff.
SRC: <https://truthout.org/articles/dhs-study-praises-occupy-sandy-with-murky-intentions/>[independent] <https://episcopalnewsservice.org/2014/03/18/federal-government-studies-occupy-sandy-movement/>[independent] <https://www.thecityreporter.nyc/2022/10/28/ten-years-occupy-sandy-disaster-response/>[independent]

## Extinction Rebellion's Self-Organising System (2018–present)
TRIED: Scale mass civil disobedience through a holacracy-inspired system: a coordination group of 8–10 people over autonomous working groups and local branches.
PROVED: It scaled fast internationally. 'The Big One' in April 2023 drew an estimated 60,000 people from 200+ organisations.
STALLED: Autonomous units acted in the movement's name against majority wishes: after Canning Town, XR said 'very few people in XR wanted this to happen'. A UK police assessment expected support to decline steadily. In January 2023 XR UK announced 'We Quit', a temporary halt to mass disruption.
LESSON: A mission portal needs a visible, legitimate way to decide what is done in the mission's name, and to stop it.
SRC: <https://qz.com/work/1776861/extinction-rebellion-is-using-holacracy-to-scale-its-international-movement>[independent] <https://theoutline.com/post/6704/out-dying-planet-cant-scream-but-people-can>[independent] <https://extinctionrebellion.uk/2022/12/31/we-quit/>[primary] <https://www.npcc.police.uk/SysSiteAssets/media/downloads/publications/disclosure-logs/operations-coordination-committee/2024/159-2024-npocc-sib-extinction-rebellion-uk-group-assessment-feb-2023-final_redacted.pdf>[independent] <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinction_Rebellion>[independent]

## Kickstarter reward crowdfunding (2009–present)
TRIED: Backers pre-fund a specific, bounded project, all-or-nothing, in exchange for promised rewards.
PROVED: A survey of 47,188 backers of 30,323 projects (2009–2015) found about 9% of funded projects failed to deliver (range 5–14%). Most money given to a concrete deliverable led to a delivery.
STALLED: The platform does not enforce delivery, so roughly 1 in 10 funded projects failed and the loss fell on backers. Kickstarter commissioned the study itself.
LESSON: Tie support to a specific deliverable and keep a public delivery record. Report delivered versus promised, not just money raised.
SRC: <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2699251>[independent] <https://www.kickstarter.com/fulfillment>[primary] <https://www.cnbc.com/2015/12/10/9-percent-kickstarter-projects-fail-to-deliver.html>[independent]

## GoFundMe medical crowdfunding (unknown start (2010s)–present)
TRIED: Let anyone raise money for a need from their own social network.
PROVED: It moves money fast for people with reach. In one US study only 9.2% of medical campaigns met their goal (mean 41.75% of goal). A 2020 analysis found a median of $265 raised, and 30% raised $0.
STALLED: Results track the income, education and network wealth of the person asking. States with more medical debt used it more but met goals less often. Black beneficiaries received smaller donations.
LESSON: When support becomes resources, it amplifies causes that are already well connected. Resources have to be matched to stated needs, not to reach.
SRC: <https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0229760>[independent] <https://www.newsweek.com/gofundme-requests-help-medical-bills-rarely-hit-their-goals-study-1676499>[independent] <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0277953623002095>[independent]

## Zooniverse micro-task citizen science (unknown start (late 2000s)–present)
TRIED: Split research into small tasks anyone can do, such as classifying an image, and combine the answers through redundancy.
PROVED: Across 7 projects over 180 days, 100,386 people gave 129,500 hours of unpaid work, worth more than $1.5M at undergraduate pay rates.
STALLED: About 10% of contributors did about 80% of the work, and most did a single task. Activity peaked at launch and then declined.
LESSON: Plan for a small core doing most of the work and a spike at launch followed by decline. Serve the core and give newcomers an easy first task.
SRC: <https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1408907112>[independent] <https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/01/150105151046.htm>[independent]

## eBird (2002–present)
TRIED: Birders submit structured checklists (place, time, effort, species) into one shared database, with automated filters on the data.
PROVED: Over 1 billion observations by May 2021 and 2 billion by June 2025. The 2025 Status update drew on 63.7M checklists from 617,711 contributors. A Science paper used 36M observations to map population declines.
STALLED: It is reliable only where data is dense: trends hold with about 10,000 checklists for an area. Quality depends on filters and historical baselines. Whether early uptake was slow was not confirmed.
LESSON: Structure at the moment of contribution, plus automated checks, turns huge numbers of casual contributions into usable, checked progress without a curator bottleneck.
SRC: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EBird>[independent] <https://ebird.org/news/2025-year-in-review>[primary] <https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2025/08/lab-ornithology-hits-2-billion-bird-sightings-3-million-recordings>[primary] <https://ebird.org/news/ebird-passes-1-billion-bird-observations>[primary]

## Humanitarian OpenStreetMap Tasking Manager (unknown start (2010s)–present)
TRIED: Split disaster mapping into grid tasks that volunteers claim, with a separate validation step done by more experienced mappers.
PROVED: After the 2015 Nepal earthquake, more than 7,000 volunteers added map data used in the relief effort.
STALLED: A study of 746 completed projects over two years found a major bottleneck between mapping and validation, because validation capacity is scarce. Quality from novice mappers was a recurring concern.
LESSON: Contributions grow with attention but checking does not. Budget and route reviewer capacity, and use automation to triage, or unchecked work piles up.
SRC: <https://zenodo.org/records/11659011>[independent] <https://www.researchgate.net/publication/325397280_Towards_Using_the_Potential_of_OpenStreetMap_History_for_Disaster_Activation_Monitoring>[independent] <https://odimpact.org/case-nepal-earthquake-recovery.html>[independent] <https://www.weforum.org/stories/2015/05/how-digital-maps-are-helping-the-nepal-rescue-effort/>[independent]

## Participatory budgeting platforms (Better Reykjavik / My Neighbourhood; Decidim Barcelona) (2011–2023 (Reykjavik PB); 2016–present (Barcelona))
TRIED: Residents propose and vote on projects, the city commits a budget in advance, and progress is followed up in public.
PROVED: Reykjavik had 608 ideas approved by 2017, with up to 450m ISK (about €3m) a year at its peak. Barcelona's 2016 action plan drew 10,000+ proposals and accepted 71% of residents' proposals; the city reports about 90% executed (its own figure).
STALLED: Barcelona's 2020–23 participatory budget was cut from €75m to €30m and delivery slipped to 2022–23. Critics cite the digital divide and neighbourhoods competing with each other. Reykjavik's last cycle was 2022–23, with no public reason given ('may restart').
LESSON: Follow-through happens when an owner with money commits in advance and publishes delivery status. It lasts only as long as that owner's budget and political will.
SRC: <https://www.citizens.is/impact/my-neighbourhood/>[primary] <https://ajuntament.barcelona.cat/innovaciodemocratica/en/decidimbarcelona>[primary] <https://participedia.net/case/decidim-participatory-budgeting-in-barcelona>[independent] <https://oecd-opsi.org/innovations/better-reykjavik/>[independent]

FAILURE MODES:
- Support is counted in clicks and signatures instead of results against a named target, and public token support can take the place of real effort.
- Checking does not grow with contributions: validators and curators become the bottleneck (HOT) and unchecked work piles up.
- A small core does most of the work (about 10% do 80%), with a spike at launch and then decline, so a platform built for 'the crowd' is really run by a few people.
- Leaderless openness with no legitimate way to decide, or to stop actions taken in the mission's name (Occupy assemblies, XR at Canning Town).
- Resources follow reach, not need: allocation weighted by support amplifies the already well connected (GoFundMe).
- Unpaid volunteers burn out once the surge passes, and there is no funded core or handoff (Occupy Sandy).
- Follow-through depends on one owner's budget and political will; when that shifts, the process shrinks or pauses (Barcelona cuts, Reykjavik pause).
- Better tools for writing contributions do not change outcomes (the AI writing tool on change.org).

NEW NOW: In 2026 AI can make the scarce middle steps much cheaper: triaging and pre-checking contributions, matching offers to open needs, and summarising for reviewers. That is the gap that sank HOT validation and burned out curators, and eBird already shows that automated filters can keep masses of casual input usable. Supporters' compute and agent work could also count as a new kind of contribution. The evidence so far is sobering. AI on change.org changed the text of petitions but not their outcomes, and cheaper contributions risk flooding the review bottleneck rather than clearing it. None of the harder problems have changed: a legitimate owner who decides and commits resources, trusted checking, resources going to need rather than reach, and a funded core that lasts after the attention spike. Pure support-proportional scaling would repeat the GoFundMe and launch-spike patterns unless the portal deliberately routes capacity to checking and to stated needs.

## SETI@home (Berkeley, on BOINC) (1999–2020 (work distribution stopped 31 Mar 2020))
TRIED: Turn idle home PCs into a search engine for radio signals from space, with volunteers donating CPU time.
PROVED: Donated compute can run for two decades on goodwill alone; the reusable platform it grew (BOINC) outlived it. Volunteer numbers at shutdown: unknown, not confirmed.
STALLED: Berkeley paused it because the science had hit diminishing returns (enough data had been analysed) and running the distributed processing took a lot of staff time. The bottleneck was the small team's back-end analysis and write-up, not volunteer supply.
LESSON: Donated capacity is only worth what the core team can turn into questions and absorb as results, so fund that team first.
SRC: <https://archive.seti.org/setihome-going-hibernation>[primary] <https://hackaday.com/2020/03/06/farewell-setihome/>[independent]

## Folding@home COVID-19 surge (2000–present; surge Mar–Apr 2020)
TRIED: Point a long-running volunteer protein-simulation network at SARS-CoV-2 drug targets when the pandemic began.
PROVED: A cause people care about turns into capacity fast: about 30,000 users in Jan 2020 grew to over 1 million devices by late March. The network reached 2.43 exaFLOPS on 12 Apr 2020, the first exaflop system. The project has 226 papers since 2000.
STALLED: The surge outran the servers that hand out work, and volunteers briefly had nothing to run. By 31 Oct 2025 capacity was 24.8 x86 petaFLOPS, about 1% of the 2020 peak, so the support faded with public attention.
LESSON: Keep ready-to-run tasks queued so a surge of help lands, and plan for most of it to leave once the headlines move on.
SRC: <https://www.tomshardware.com/news/folding-at-home-breaks-exaflop-barrier-fight-coronavirus-covid-19>[independent] <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folding@home>[independent]

## Prime Intellect INTELLECT-1 / -2 / -3 (2024–present)
TRIED: Train open AI models on compute pooled over the internet from many providers instead of one company's cluster.
PROVED: INTELLECT-1 (Nov 2024) was a 10B-parameter model trained on 1T tokens. It ran on up to 112 H100 GPUs in 5 countries, with 30 independent providers joining and leaving during the run. The GPUs stayed busy 83% of the time globally and 96% within the US.
STALLED: The flagship INTELLECT-3 (2025) moved to a single cluster of 512 H200s with fast InfiniBand links (about $2.2M of compute). Its reinforcement-learning training needed tight synchronisation that ordinary internet links could not deliver.
LESSON: Donated compute fits loosely coupled work (inference, evaluation, independent agent runs, batch jobs); tightly coupled work drifts back to one data centre, so design the tasks around that.
SRC: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01152>[primary] <https://www.implicator.ai/prime-intellects-intellect-3-open-source-ambition-meets-centralized-reality/>[independent]

## Bittensor (TAO) (2021–present)
TRIED: Pay a token to 'miners' who produce AI outputs, scored by 'validators', across many task-specific sub-networks.
PROVED: Token rewards draw a lot of capacity and capital: 129 subnets by mid-2025. A mid-2025 overview measured activity in staking and price, not verified useful output.
STALLED: Some validators copied other validators' scores instead of checking the work, which the project's own 2024 paper documents. A tampered release of its Python package (6.12.2) led to an $8M theft in July 2024, and the chain was put into 'safe mode' with transactions stopped.
LESSON: When rewards are tradeable and checking work is expensive, people fake the checking, so make verification the core product and not an afterthought.
SRC: <https://docs.learnbittensor.org/papers/BT_Weight_Copier-29May2024.pdf>[primary] <https://www.coindesk.com/tech/2024/07/03/bittensors-tao-slides-15-after-8m-wallet-drain-attack>[independent] <https://emoryblockchain.substack.com/p/state-of-bittensor-in-2025>[independent]

## Gitcoin Grants (quadratic funding) (2019–present (Grants Stack wound down 2025))
TRIED: Match small donations from a pool so that how many people back a project counts more than how much each gives.
PROVED: Over $50M reached 3,715 projects through 3.8M unique donations. GG23 still distributed over $1.3M through quadratic and retroactive funding.
STALLED: Because the number of backers drives the match, fake accounts pay, and Gitcoin had to add identity scoring (Passport) and discounting of coordinated donor clusters. Its round-running software (Grants Stack) was wound down in 2025; a secondary review attributes this to unsustainable unit economics.
LESSON: Weighting by number of supporters needs defences against fake and colluding supporters from day one, and the platform itself needs revenue that doesn't come out of the causes it serves.
SRC: <https://gitcoin.co/program>[primary] <https://gitcoin.co/apps/gitcoin-grants-stack>[primary] <https://gitcoin.co/blog/gitcoin-grants-23-retro>[primary] <https://cryptoadventure.com/gitcoin-review-2026-grants-human-passport-and-public-goods-funding/>[independent]

## Optimism Retroactive Public Goods Funding, Round 3 (2021–present; Round 3 in 2023)
TRIED: Pay contributors after the fact for impact already delivered, with a panel of human voters ('badgeholders') deciding the amounts.
PROVED: 30M OP went to 501 recipients, up from 195 in the previous round. It showed retroactive payment can reach a long tail of contributors.
STALLED: 145 badgeholders faced 643 eligible applicants, and 967 of 1,594 submissions were excluded. Reviewing took a median 16 hours per badgeholder and hundreds for some. Applicants' claims were self-reported and unverifiable, and quorum rules pushed applicants to lobby for votes. Rewards barely separated projects (top 1% got about 6x the median), so big impact went under-rewarded. Later rounds narrowed scope and moved to measured impact data.
LESSON: Human juries stop working at a few hundred items, so give them narrow scopes and verifiable impact data, not self-reported claims.
SRC: <https://optimism.io/blog/retropgf-3-learnings-reflections>[primary] <https://optimism.io/blog/announcing-retropgf-round-3-recipients>[primary]

## Deep Funding (AI proposes, jury spot-checks) (2025–present)
TRIED: Let competing AI models propose funding weights across Ethereum's software dependency graph (40,000+ links), scored against a human jury's random spot checks.
PROVED: $250k from Vitalik Buterin launched it. AI models filled in the graph cheaply: one model's 612 synthetic comparisons connected it where jurors' did not.
STALLED: The human 'ground truth' was thin and noisy. In round 1, 37 jurors made 407 comparisons; 78.5% of pairs were judged by a single juror, and removing any one juror moved weights by 33.5% on average. Adding juror comparisons did not improve model scores.
LESSON: AI makes proposals cheap, but the scarce input is still consistent human judgment, so gather it deliberately, with overlap between reviewers, and record how certain each result is.
SRC: <https://gitcoin.co/mechanisms/deep-funding>[primary] <https://ethereum-magicians.org/t/model-submissions-for-ethereum-deep-funding/24200>[primary]

## Open Collective and Open Collective Foundation (OCF) (platform ongoing; OCF fiscal hosting ended 30 Sep 2024, dissolved 31 Dec 2024)
TRIED: Give groups without a legal entity public, transparent budgets, with a nonprofit 'fiscal host' holding the money on their behalf.
PROVED: Public, line-by-line budgets are workable at scale: OCF alone hosted about 600 collectives. The platform continues under the Open Finance Consortium (OFiCo), run by organisations that use it.
STALLED: OCF ran at a deficit because its 5% fee did not cover compliance and payouts that were often reviewed by hand. When it dissolved, 600+ mutual-aid, open-source and tenant groups lost their host and had to spend down, move or return funds.
LESSON: The expensive layer is legal custody and compliance, not the public ledger, so partner for it or price it honestly.
SRC: <https://opencollective.com/foundation/updates/announcement-we-are-dissolving-open-collective-foundation-at-the-end-of-this-year>[primary] <https://blog.opencollective.com/open-collective-official-statement-ocf-dissolution/>[primary] <https://communityovercode.com/2024/02/open-collective-foundation-shutdown-explainer/>[independent]

## Token-governed treasuries: The DAO and Aragon (The DAO 2016; Aragon Association dissolved Nov 2023)
TRIED: Put a mission's money under token-holder votes instead of a board.
PROVED: Supporters will pool large sums fast: The DAO raised about $150M in ETH. Aragon's association built up an 86,343 ETH (about $155M) treasury.
STALLED: The DAO: a bug in its code let an attacker drain 3.6M ETH (about $50M). The fix, a hard fork on 20 Jul 2016, split Ethereum into two chains, and the SEC later deemed the tokens securities. Aragon: the promised handover of the treasury to token-holder control never happened. In 2023 traders who buy tokens to capture treasuries (the 'Risk Free Value' raiders, led by Arca) bought ANT to outvote the mission, so the association dissolved and paid out the treasury.
LESSON: If a stake can be bought, a big treasury draws buyers who want the money rather than the mission, so tie a say in direction to contribution and backing, not to tradeable tokens.

## VitaDAO (decentralized science, longevity) (founding date unconfirmed; active 2022–present)
TRIED: Have a token-governed community fund and co-own early longevity research.
PROVED: A community can source and fund science deals and bring in institutional money: 19 projects and about $4M deployed, a $4.1M round in Jan 2023 including Pfizer Ventures, and its first spinout company.
STALLED: Sources report dollars and projects funded, not scientific results; this research found no confirmed therapeutic outcome. About $4M is small against what drug development costs.
LESSON: Count results, not money raised: show on the portal which questions were answered and what was checked, not just what was funded.
SRC: <https://www.theblock.co/post/255164/pfizer-backed-dao-launches-community-funded-biotech-firm>[independent] <https://www.coindesk.com/web3/2023/01/30/vitadao-closes-41m-funding-round-with-pfizer-ventures-for-longevity-research>[independent] <https://www.vitadao.com/blog-article/announcing-the-first-spinout-company-funded-by-vitadao>[primary]

FAILURE MODES:
- Attention fades: support that arrives with the headlines leaves with them (Folding@home kept about 1% of its 2020 peak by 2025).
- The project can't take in the help: capacity arrives faster than the core team can create tasks or check results (Folding@home ran out of work to hand out; SETI@home was paused partly because running it took so much staff time).
- Nobody checks the work: when rewards are paid for claimed work, people fake it or copy others' checking (Bittensor validators copying scores; Optimism applicants' unverifiable self-reported claims).
- Fake and colluding supporters: counting backers invites fake accounts and coordinated donors, which need identity and anti-collusion defences (Gitcoin Passport and cluster matching).
- Judges run out of time: human judgment stops scaling at a few hundred items (a median 16 hours per Optimism badgeholder; in Deep Funding 78.5% of pairs were seen by one juror).
- Popularity over impact: support-weighted votes reward lobbying and visibility, and rewards end up too flat (Optimism's top 1% got only about 6x the median).
- Money as the prize: a tradeable stake plus a large treasury draws people who want the money and eventually capture the governance (Aragon's 'Risk Free Value' raiders).
- Custody costs are underpriced: legally holding money for many small groups costs more than a thin fee brings in (the Open Collective Foundation's 5% fee led to its dissolution).
- The platform can't pay for itself: the software the support flows through isn't funded by that support (Gitcoin's Grants Stack wound down).
- Tightly coupled work pulls compute back to one data centre (INTELLECT-3 moved to a single 512-GPU cluster).
- One exploit can take everything when code or a package holds the money (The DAO's $50M drain; Bittensor's $8M theft through a tampered package).

NEW NOW: What has changed since these attempts, soberly:
(1) AI agents can now do many small, checkable pieces of work almost for free. That turns 'more supporters' into real capacity: people can sponsor agent runs and donate inference, which is exactly the loosely coupled work INTELLECT-1 showed pooled compute can handle, at 83–96% utilisation. It also moves the bottleneck from doing the work to checking it.
(2) Deep Funding shows AI can propose how to divide money or attention across thousands of items, but the human judgment it is checked against stays thin and noisy. A single juror can shift weights by about 33%.
(3) Defences against fake supporters (Passport, cluster matching) and measured impact data (Open Source Observer, used by Optimism) have matured, but mostly for code, where impact is easy to measure. My inference, not sourced: AI also makes fake supporters and plausible fake work cheaper.
What has not changed: attention fades, curator and juror time is scarce, holding money legally is expensive, and a tradeable stake draws people who want the treasury.
Net: the odds improve only for a portal that
- counts support mainly as attention, compute and checked work, not tradeable stakes;
- keeps work queued and ready to absorb surges;
- makes checking the product (source-backed claims, several independent reviewers on the same item, recorded confidence);
- partners for legal custody of funds instead of building it.
Nothing found shows a mission-owning portal that has sustained this outside crypto-native or code-centric communities.
