# Value hypotheses, round 2: developing a topic

The primary use (per the product owner, 23 September 2026): **a person or a group looks
into a topic, explores it, develops it over time, and contributes to it — with delegation
and collaboration, to people and to AI agents.** Not crisis response, not logistics.

"Topic" means anything someone is working out: a research question, a design, a local
history, a policy idea, a product direction, a build, a body of practice.

Each hypothesis is a claim that could be false. Value means: a real person gets an
outcome they cannot reasonably get with tools they already have or could adopt cheaply.

## What exists today (do not credit the product with more)

Built, synthetic data only: free-text offers matched to written needs with a confidence
gate, specific replies on the sender's channel, capacity that cannot be double-booked, a
coordinator queue that records corrections, outbound asks, a public page of needs closing.
Not built: any record of findings, questions or decisions; any exploration or
conversation interface; audiences; agent delegation.

## Already learned in round 1 (do not re-research)

- Meeting and chat AI already extracts decisions and action items and links them to the
  transcript moment (Google Meet, Teams Facilitator). Gaps that remain: a confirmed
  "agreed" state separate from AI extraction; one record across meetings, chat, email and
  messengers; correcting an item while keeping its history.
- Private, team and public in one tool already exists (Notion). Per-item AI exclusion is
  not something small groups ask for.
- A solo "think out loud and keep what matters" tool is a crowded market.

## The hypotheses

**T1 — Understanding doesn't accumulate.** Exploring a topic through AI chats and
conversations produces understanding that stays in long threads. People cannot easily see
what has been established versus speculated, or resume weeks later. Projects, memory and
source notebooks do not solve this well enough for sustained work on a topic.

**T2 — No living account of a topic.** The current state of a topic — what is known and
from where, what is open, what is contested, what was decided, what is being tried — is
not a first-class, correctable object anywhere. Documents and wikis flatten uncertainty
and disagreement into prose; chat loses it.

**T3 — Contributing through open questions.** Outside software, there is no common way
for others to contribute to someone's topic by taking an open question, adding evidence or
a perspective, or trying something — with their contribution visibly changing the state
and credited. (GitHub issues and pull requests do this for code; that is the benchmark.)

**T4 — Delegated work has nowhere to land.** People now delegate sub-questions to AI
agents (deep-research tools, agents) and to other people, but results come back as
reports, chats or messages, not as checked, sourced additions to a shared state. It is
hard to see what an agent actually established, and to keep several agents and people
consistent over time.

**T5 — From private to shared without starting over.** A topic often starts private,
becomes a small group's, and sometimes becomes open. Moving between these today means
switching tools and losing lineage.

**T6 — Several perspectives kept.** Groups working on contested or interpretive topics
(culture, history, policy, science) need disagreement and minority accounts kept, not
merged into one consensus page, and no mainstream tool supports this well.
